Chapter 01 of 17

The Boy Who Was Different

I was always the kid who never quite fit in. Not because I was introverted or shy — quite the opposite.

I was loud, present, always needing stimulation and action. But somehow I was always one step to the side of everyone else. A little too much. A little too different. Too intense for rooms that preferred silence. Too real for a world that rewarded conformity.

My parents raised us well. Healthy food, lots of travel, experiences instead of gifts. As a child I had no understanding for this. The others had Gameboys. New sneakers. I had brown apples from the garden and parents who told me memories were worth more. I hated that then. Today I'm grateful for it.

I remember the day at carnival.

The whole class had agreed to come in costume. My father and I had spent hours preparing a leopard costume — full makeup, tail, the whole program. I was proud. More than proud. I was convinced I had the best costume in the history of the world.

When I arrived at school, I was the only one. One day too early.

I stood there — nine or ten years old — in my leopard costume, with makeup on my face, while everyone stared at me. I ran to the basement and cried until a classmate came down and got me.

The rest of the day I sat in class with my little leopard tail. Makeup cried off. Silence.

What I didn't understand then: that moment had nothing to do with the costume. It was the first real lesson of my life about what it feels like to be the only one. To be visible when everyone else prefers to be invisible.

Years later, this exact quality would carry me onto runways in Los Angeles and New York. Into castings, boardrooms and TV studios. Onto stages worldwide.

But in that school basement, all of that was still very far away. I came back upstairs. I sat in the front row. In full costume, with cried-off makeup, and a silent decision: I will not hide myself.

That cost me a lot of courage then. But it was the first time I consciously decided: my difference is not a weakness. It's my asset.

What I learned from this

Your "being different" is not a flaw — it's your capital

The moments when you're most ashamed of who you are often become the moments that carry you furthest. The problem is never being different. The problem is stopping to be it.

  • Who taught you that you're too much? And why are you still listening to that voice?
  • Visibility has a cost. But invisibility costs more — it costs you your life.
  • You don't need to convince everyone. You just need to stop bending yourself for others.
Chapter 02 of 17

Pushing Limits: The Military

Before the modeling story begins, there's a story I rarely talk about in this depth. The German military. More specifically: the paratroopers.

I'd signed up because I'd always been into extreme sports and wanted to test my limits. Sounds simple. It wasn't. The paratrooper training is the toughest in the German military after the combat swimmers.

The first day started at 4:50 AM. Not wake-up. Not breakfast. Drill. Every day until 10 PM. Three months straight.

The hardest part wasn't the shooting. It came three months later. We were woken at 3:30 AM. No lights. Packing in complete darkness. Thrown into military trucks — nine men, ninety minutes through nowhere, two degrees, constant rain.

What followed were three days I will never forget. I lay in the mud next to the camp with my buddy — alarm post duty. Not allowed to fall asleep. The cold crept slowly through the clothing. At some point it stops hurting — because you're too numb to feel anything.

I lay there in the mud thinking about my warm bed at home. About everything that didn't exist right now.

And then something happened. At some point I stopped fighting against it.

You can't fight against everything. Sometimes you just have to be. That was my first conscious encounter with the idea that would shape me as a coach years later.

At the end of those three days: campfire, beer, food. The "punishment" was a surprise. We received our licenses. I cried in that moment. Not in front of everyone. But I cried.

Because I understood: your body can do more than your mind believes. Always. This insight saved my life. More than once.

What I learned from this

Your body knows more than your mind — listen to it

We're so trained to mentally rationalize and plan that we forget our body carries its own wisdom.

  • Stress is not your enemy. It's a signal.
  • When you want to give up: that's the moment right before the breakthrough.
  • Life tests you — not to break you, but to show you what you carry.
Chapter 07 of 17 — The most dramatic night

Los Angeles: The Villa, The Fraud, The Pitbull Nights

Los Angeles. The city of dreams. Also the city where dreams can hit you so hard you don't know whether to laugh or cry.

I flew over, coached the photographer for a few weeks, went to Miami and New York. Then: LA, modeling agencies, castings. And there I met Elijah.

He said he'd sign me. I'd get a room in his villa in Beverly Hills. He'd organize the artist visa. I thought: this sounds too good to be true. It was too good to be true.

But at first everything fit. The villa was real. I walked in the LA Fashion Week — and then in New York. Both fashion weeks. If you've ever had the feeling of being on the right path — that was that moment for me.

Then I flew back to LA. On the plane my phone rang. Elijah. "Call me when you land." I landed. I called. The house in Beverly Hills was sold. Money problems. I should come to the hotel.

11 PM. LA. I didn't know where to go. At the hotel: Elijah, another model, two beds, three pitbulls. "Sleep with the dogs, I'll have a new house soon."

Next morning: the bed was wet. The dogs had pissed on my bed. I stood at the window, watched the two of them in the car below — they were rolling the window down to let out the bong smoke — and thought only:

What is happening to my life right now?

That went on for a week. Every night the same. Then I started researching. Elijah Adams. DNR list. "Do Not Rent." A childhood photo he'd shown us — I found it online. Next to an obituary.

This person had been dead for ten years. Elijah had been using the identity of a deceased person.

I packed my things quietly while the two sat in the car. Disappeared. And landed a few days later in a real villa in Woodland Hills — through contacts I'd built in LA. My wife visited me. We slept in that villa and everything was, for one moment, exactly how I'd imagined it.

I'd loved LA. And I'd hated LA. I'd seen how thin facades can be. But I'd also seen what's possible when you dare.

What I learned from this

Spot the facade — and don't build one yourself

  • If something sounds too good to be true — it usually is.
  • Network is not who you know. It's who's there for you when the villa is gone.
  • Real relationships prove themselves when the bed is wet and you don't know where to go.
